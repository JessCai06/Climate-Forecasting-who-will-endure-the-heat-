    Historical Data: Temperature

    U.S. summers already run hotter than the grid was built for, and nights are warming fastest.

    Trends

    • Cooling degree days: +19% in the median city, 1976–2005 to the 2019–2025 forecast; up in 193 of 197 cities.
    • Days with a high of 95 °F or more: +21%. Nights with a low of 75 °F or more: +48%.

    Findings

    • The rise lands where it is already hot: Texas, the Gulf Coast, the Southwest.
    • Our forecast ran low against what happened, so these numbers are conservative.

    Historical Data: Energy Use

    Every 1 °F hotter day adds about 2% to peak electricity demand, in every U.S. region.

    Trends

    • U.S.: +13,700 MW of daily peak per 1 °F of the daily high (2.2%), summers 2015–2018.
    • Most sensitive: Florida, 3.4% per °F. Least: the Northwest, 1.2%. Warmer nights lift the overnight floor about 1.5% per °F.

    Findings

    • Texas's longer record (2002–2018) agrees: 1.9% per °F.
    • Four regions also peaked in winter in 2016–2018: grid risk runs both ways.

    Historical Data: Energy Affordability

    Before any warming, low-income electricity bills were already over the affordable line in 45 of 46 states.

    Trends

    • Real price rose from 11.8¢ (2002) to 12.8¢ per kWh (2018); the average home used about 11,000 kWh a year.
    • Hotter years did not raise prices: −0.002¢ per 100 extra cooling degree days.

    Findings

    • Single person, low income: over the bar in 45 of 46 states. Family of four: 36 of 46.
    • Worst where prices are high (New England, New York, California) or use is high (the South).

    What the Three Datasets Tell Us

    Hot summers raise how much power people use; what it costs follows fuel markets, not the weather.

    Trends

    • Year to year, a hotter summer means more use per home (r = 0.60 for year-to-year changes, 2002–2018).
    • Price does not rise in hot years (r = −0.36); use per home drifted down after 2010 as appliances got more efficient.

    Findings

    • Unaffordability is structural: income, price and baseline use.
    • Heat's cost lands on the grid at the peak, where reserves are planned on past weather.

    Projected Numbers, 2019–2025

    Summers keep getting hotter, but efficiency outpaces the extra cooling, and nothing in the record says prices will follow the heat.

    Trends

    • Weather: cooling degree days average 1,531 in 2019–2025, +8% on 2002–2018 (1,420).
    • Use per home: the extra heat adds about 95 kWh a year (+0.9%), but the efficiency trend (−28 kWh a year) brings use down to about 10,700 kWh.

    Findings

    • Price is held at 2018's 12.8¢: in 2002–2018 it moved with fuel markets, not weather.
    • So the average bill barely moves; the heat shows up at the peak and in the regions, not in the national average.

    The Questions We Are Asking

    Our forecast shows where today’s plans fall short. What to do about it is a choice for the people who hold each lever, so pick a role to see the question we hand them and the evidence behind it.

    The risk we noticed

    More very hot days and hot nights, and hotter afternoon peaks. Compared with 1976–2005, our 2019–2025 forecast has 21% more days of 95 °F or more and 48% more nights of 75 °F or more. The grid feels it at the peak; homes feel it where cooling is missing or costs too much to run.

    Who they are: the groups that decide how much spare power the grid must keep ready for the hottest afternoons, and the utilities that build to meet it.

    What we found

    Grids are planned with a cushion of spare capacity, and that cushion was sized on past weather. By 2025 the warming we forecast uses up about a fifth of it. In a hot summer, Texas’s main grid falls 1.3% short of what people would want to use.

    Demand on the hottest days climbs about 2% for every degree of extra heat, so small shifts in temperature matter more than they sound.

    The questions we are asking

    • Should the spare-capacity target be built on forecast weather instead of the weather of the past?
    • How much extra margin is worth paying for, and where does it matter first?
    • Is it cheaper to build more supply, or to shrink the peak by asking users to use less at that hour?

    Where margins were already thin, the questions are the most urgent.

    The evidence

    • WeatherMore very hot days and hot nights are coming.
    • Energy UseHow much more power each degree of heat costs the grid.
    • Projected NumbersHow much of the cushion warming uses up.
    • LimitationsWhat we could not measure about the grid.

    Who they are: the state bodies that approve what electricity costs and how it is charged, and the power companies that bring those rules to life.

    What we found

    The problem with bills is not the heat. A low-income person living alone pays more than is considered affordable in 45 of 46 states, by about $300 a year. A low-income family of four is over the line in 36.

    The extra heat we forecast adds only $17–33 a year. And in 2002–2018, hotter years did not push prices up; fuel markets moved them.

    The questions we are asking

    • Should what a household pays depend on what it earns, and not only on how much power it uses?
    • Should usage rules protect people who cannot cut back, such as those who need cooling to stay safe?
    • How should the cost of meeting the hottest hours be shared among customers?

    Heat makes this old gap a little wider. It did not create it.

    Who they are: the federal and state programs that help households pay their power bills, and the agencies that run efficiency upgrades for low-income homes.

    What we found

    Help with energy bills has long meant winter heating. Yet the same households struggle in summer, and the gap above the affordable line is large everywhere, not only in the hottest states: heat exposure barely predicts which states are worst off.

    Making cooling in low-income homes use less power would ease the grid at its peak, lower the bill, and bring cooling to homes that have none.

    The questions we are asking

    • Should aid cover summer cooling as seriously as winter heating?
    • Is paying for lasting efficiency upgrades a better use of money than paying each month’s bill?
    • Which households and places should be served first?

    Upgrades need money up front but cut the peak instead of paying for new power plants. That trade is theirs to weigh.

    The evidence

    • Energy AffordabilityThe size of the gap for low-income households.
    • Regional AffordabilityHeat exposure does not line up with bill strain.
    • Energy UseWhy a lower peak helps everyone.

    Who they are: city and state officials who set housing standards, protect tenants, and run emergency response when heat is dangerous.

    What we found

    Homes fail in two different ways. In the North and West, many homes simply have no cooling: a third in Washington State, about a quarter in Maine and Montana.

    In the South, nearly every home has it, but hot nights keep climbing (Florida from 69 to 96 a year, Texas from 46 to 75). There the risk is a bill too high to run it, or the power going out.

    The questions we are asking

    • Should renters be guaranteed a safe indoor temperature?
    • Should power stay on for households during dangerous heat, even when a bill is unpaid?
    • Where should public cooling places be, and when should they open?

    The right mix differs by region, so one national rule would fit neither.

    How We Will Answer It

    One concrete proposal for each group, built so that each helps the others. Pick a role to see what it would do, who else it affects and what supports it.

    Who acts: regional grid operators and the state bodies that approve utility plans.

    Our proposal

    • Plan the spare capacity for the hottest afternoons on forecast weather, not only past weather, by adding a warming allowance to each region’s summer peak estimate.
    • Count peak-cutting as capacity: efficient cooling, and agreements to use less at the peak hour, should compete on equal terms with new power plants.
    • Start where the cushion was already thin, such as Texas.

    What it does for the other sides

    • Households: fewer outages in a heat wave.
    • Rate regulators: the allowance shows up in bills, so cutting the peak is the cheaper first move.
    • Aid funders: efficient cooling in low-income homes becomes a source of capacity, not only a social program.

    The evidence

    • Auffhammer et al. 2017Peak demand is projected to grow faster than average use as it warms.
    • IEA 2018More efficient cooling can cut the need for new power plants at the peak.
    • Energy UseWhat each degree of heat costs the grid.

    Who acts: the state bodies that approve electricity prices, and the power companies that apply them.

    Our proposal

    • Bring low-income bills down to the affordable line with an income-based discount, so no household pays more than about 6% of its income for home energy.
    • Spread the cost across all customers, so it stays small for each one.
    • Pair the discount with efficiency upgrades, so it can shrink over time.

    What it does for the other sides

    • Households: smaller bills, fewer shut-offs.
    • Other customers: pay a little more, and gain a steadier grid.
    • Utilities: fewer unpaid bills.
    • Grid planners: the paired upgrades lower the peak.

    The evidence

    • Borenstein 2012Direct, income-based help wastes less than discounting through tiered prices.
    • Energy AffordabilityHow far over the line low-income bills are.
    • Regional AffordabilityWhere the gap is largest.

    Who acts: the federal and state programs that help pay power bills, and the agencies that run efficiency upgrades.

    Our proposal

    • Treat summer cooling like winter heating: let aid help pay cooling bills, not only heating.
    • Put part of the money into lasting upgrades for low-income homes (efficient heat pumps, insulation, sealing), starting with homes that have no cooling or very old equipment.
    • Run small trials first and measure the real savings before scaling up.

    What it does for the other sides

    • Grid planners: a lower peak.
    • Rate regulators: lower bills mean a smaller discount to fund.
    • Cities: homes that had no cooling get it.
    • Households: lower bills that stay lower.

    The evidence

    • Deetjen et al. 2021In 55 U.S. cities, heat pumps cut emissions damages in about 7 in 10 homes and saved money in about 1 in 3.
    • Barreca et al. 2016Home air conditioning explains nearly all of the fall in heat deaths since 1960.
    • Energy AffordabilityThe gap this money would target.

    Who acts: city and state officials who set housing rules, protect tenants and run emergency response.

    Our proposal

    • Set a safe upper indoor temperature for rented homes. A limit near 82 °F is a starting point that local data can adjust.
    • Keep the power on for households during dangerously hot days, working with the state body that sets utility rules.
    • Open public cooling places on forecast hot days and hot nights, placed using our forecast.

    What it does for the other sides

    • Households: a safer home, and a safer night.
    • Landlords: take on upgrade costs, which aid funders can help cover.
    • Utilities: some bills are paid later, in exchange for fewer emergencies.
    • Aid funders: a clear list of homes to reach first.

    The evidence

    • Barreca et al. 2016Where homes have cooling, very hot days kill far fewer people.
    • WeatherHot days and nights by place.
    • DefinitionsWhat “safe to live in” means here.

    The thread that runs through all four

    Use less power at the hottest hour. Efficient cooling in low-income homes cuts the peak the grid must meet, the bill a family must pay, and the number of homes with no cooling at all. Each group can start with powers it already has, and each proposal is built to help the others.

    Why It Lasts

    A plan is sustainable if it helps now without making emissions or costs worse later. We think this one does, as long as the cooling is efficient.

    Emissions

    Cooling gets more efficient, not just bigger.

    Cooling demand will climb as the world warms and more households install air conditioning (Davis and Gertler 2015). Efficiency is the biggest lever against that: strong efficiency policy could cut the world’s cooling energy use by 45% by 2050 compared with doing nothing (IEA 2018). In 55 U.S. cities, heat pumps reduced emissions damages in about 7 in 10 homes (Deetjen et al. 2021).

    Could fail if the extra power comes from a dirty grid, or old equipment is swapped for a unit that is hardly better.

    Costs

    Cutting the peak is cheaper than building for it.

    Under a high-warming path, peak demand is projected to grow about twice as fast as average use (Auffhammer et al. 2017), and the grid must be sized for the peak. Efficient cooling reduces the need for new power plants at that hour, and the same agency estimates it lowers total investment and running costs by about $3 trillion worldwide to 2050 (IEA 2018).

    Could fail if savings come in below predictions: replaced refrigerators saved a quarter of the predicted amount (Davis et al. 2014), and Michigan home upgrades cost about twice the energy they saved (Fowlie et al. 2018). That is why trials come first.

    Over the long run

    Keeps cooling a safety net as hot days multiply.

    Home air conditioning explains nearly all of the 75% fall in the death toll of very hot days in the U.S. since 1960 (Barreca et al. 2016). With more very hot days and nights coming, that protection holds only if homes have cooling, can afford to run it, and the grid stays on. The four proposals aim at exactly those three conditions.

    Could fail if funding fades, or warming outruns the forecast. We would revisit the plan each year with new forecasts and trial results.

    Limitations

    Warming is a modest part of grid risk to 2025. Here is what we assumed, what our data cannot see, and what would change the answer.

    Where the 2025 grid margin goes, median NERC area

    Percentage points of reserve margin lost

    Warming (our forecast)−1.6

    One hot summer (about 1 in 10)−8.4

    What we assumed

    • NERC’s 50/50 peak forecasts do not already include warming.
    • Demand rises linearly with heat; the peak day’s high stands in for the peak hour.
    • A higher price in scarcity is only a scenario: hot years did not raise prices in 2002–2018.

    What our data cannot see

    • No humidity: dry-heat thresholds understate risk in humid places.
    • Airport stations miss city-centre heat; EIA-930 covers only 3.5 summers.
    • RECS income brackets approximate poverty; Alaska and Hawaii are left out.

    Outside our scope

    • The cold side: four regions hit their yearly peak in winter in 2016–2018. Grid risk runs both ways.
    • Load is what customers drew, so demand cut in emergencies does not show.

    What would change the conclusion

    Plans already include warming. Then the planning gap shrinks.

    The poor cut cooling more. Likely: bills fall but homes get hotter, so livability is worse than shown.

    Our forecast runs low. It was conservative on heat days, so warming’s effects are probably understated.

    Conclusion

    Not everyone will endure the heat on plans built for yesterday’s weather.

    Grids and household budgets are planned on past summers. Our forecast for 2019–2025, made only from data up to 2018, shows where that falls short.

    +48%

    The heat is uneven

    Hot nights rise by half and cooling-required days by a fifth, mostly in Texas, the Gulf Coast and the Southwest. Nights warm faster than days.

    ⅕

    The grid feels it first

    Warming uses up about a fifth of the planned reserve cushion. ERCOT, already below its reference margin, runs short in a hot summer.

    45 of 46

    Bills were already too high

    Low-income electricity bills were over the affordable bar in 45 of 46 states before any warming. Heat adds only $17–33 a year.

    So

    Plan reserves on forecast weather, fix affordability where it is structural, and bring cooling to homes that have none. One lever serves all three: efficient cooling for low-income homes, which lowers the peak, lowers the bill and makes homes livable.
