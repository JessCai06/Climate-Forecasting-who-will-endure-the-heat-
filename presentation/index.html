    Definitions

    Two lines decide whether a home can endure the heat: what a family can afford, and what a body can bear.

    Historical Data: Temperature

    U.S. summers already run hotter than the grid was built for, and nights are warming fastest.

    Trends

    • Cooling degree days: +19% in the median city, 1976–2005 to the 2019–2025 forecast; up in 193 of 197 cities.
    • Days with a high of 95 °F or more: +21%. Nights with a low of 75 °F or more: +48%.

    Findings

    • The rise lands where it is already hot: Texas, the Gulf Coast, the Southwest.
    • Our forecast ran low against what happened, so these numbers are conservative.

    Historical Data: Energy Use

    Every 1 °F hotter day adds about 2% to peak electricity demand, in every U.S. region.

    Trends

    • U.S.: +13,700 MW of daily peak per 1 °F of the daily high (2.2%), summers 2015–2018.
    • Most sensitive: Florida, 3.4% per °F. Least: the Northwest, 1.2%. Warmer nights lift the overnight floor about 1.5% per °F.

    Findings

    • Texas's longer record (2002–2018) agrees: 1.9% per °F.
    • Four regions also peaked in winter in 2016–2018: grid risk runs both ways.

    Historical Data: Energy Affordability

    Before any warming, low-income electricity bills were already over the affordable line in 45 of 46 states.

    Trends

    • Real price rose from 11.8¢ (2002) to 12.8¢ per kWh (2018); the average home used about 11,000 kWh a year.
    • Hotter years did not raise prices: −0.002¢ per 100 extra cooling degree days.

    Findings

    • Single person, low income: over the bar in 45 of 46 states. Family of four: 36 of 46.
    • Worst where prices are high (New England, New York, California) or use is high (the South).

    What the Three Datasets Tell Us

    Hot summers raise how much power people use; what it costs follows fuel markets, not the weather.

    Trends

    • Year to year, a hotter summer means more use per home (r = 0.60 for year-to-year changes, 2002–2018).
    • Price does not rise in hot years (r = −0.36); use per home drifted down after 2010 as appliances got more efficient.

    Findings

    • Unaffordability is structural: income, price and baseline use.
    • Heat's cost lands on the grid at the peak, where reserves are planned on past weather.
