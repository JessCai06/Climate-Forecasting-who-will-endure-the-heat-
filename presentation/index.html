Will Everyone Endure the Heat?

Many U.S. cities face heat they were not built for, and the families least able to pay for cooling feel it first.

    Definitions

    Two lines decide whether a home can endure the heat: what a family can afford, and what a body can bear.

    Affordable
    6% of income on home energy · median state, single person, bill ÷ bar
    Livable
    82 °F indoors at most · hot nights a year
    Cooling required
    95 °F outside: fans stop protecting · days a year
    “ A home is affordable when a household at the poverty line spends no more than 6% of its income keeping the power on, and livable when it stays at or below 82 °F inside. ”

    Historical Data: Temperature

    U.S. summers already run hotter than the grid was built for, and nights are warming fastest.

    Trends

    • Cooling degree days: +19% in the median city, 1976–2005 to the 2019–2025 forecast; up in 193 of 197 cities.
    • Days with a high of 95 °F or more: +21%. Nights with a low of 75 °F or more: +48%.

    Findings

    • The rise lands where it is already hot: Texas, the Gulf Coast, the Southwest.
    • Our forecast ran low against what happened, so these numbers are conservative.

    Historical Data: Energy Use

    Every 1 °F hotter day adds about 2% to peak electricity demand, in every U.S. region.

    Trends

    • U.S.: +13,700 MW of daily peak per 1 °F of the daily high (2.2%), summers 2015–2018.
    • Most sensitive: Florida, 3.4% per °F. Least: the Northwest, 1.2%. Warmer nights lift the overnight floor about 1.5% per °F.

    Findings

    • Texas's longer record (2002–2018) agrees: 1.9% per °F.
    • Four regions also peaked in winter in 2016–2018: grid risk runs both ways.

    Historical Data: Energy Affordability

    Before any warming, low-income electricity bills were already over the affordable line in 45 of 46 states.

    Trends

    • Real price rose from 11.8¢ (2002) to 12.8¢ per kWh (2018); the average home used about 11,000 kWh a year.
    • Hotter years did not raise prices: −0.002¢ per 100 extra cooling degree days.

    Findings

    • Single person, low income: over the bar in 45 of 46 states. Family of four: 36 of 46.
    • Worst where prices are high (New England, New York, California) or use is high (the South).

    What the Three Datasets Tell Us

    Hot summers raise how much power people use; what it costs follows fuel markets, not the weather.

    Trends

    • Year to year, a hotter summer means more use per home (r = 0.60 for year-to-year changes, 2002–2018).
    • Price does not rise in hot years (r = −0.36); use per home drifted down after 2010 as appliances got more efficient.

    Findings

    • Unaffordability is structural: income, price and baseline use.
    • Heat's cost lands on the grid at the peak, where reserves are planned on past weather.

    Projected Numbers, 2019–2025

    Summers keep getting hotter, but efficiency outpaces the extra cooling, and nothing in the record says prices will follow the heat.

    Trends

    • Weather: cooling degree days average 1,531 in 2019–2025, +8% on 2002–2018 (1,420).
    • Use per home: the extra heat adds about 95 kWh a year (+0.9%), but the efficiency trend (−28 kWh a year) brings use down to about 10,700 kWh.

    Findings

    • Price is held at 2018's 12.8¢: in 2002–2018 it moved with fuel markets, not weather.
    • So the average bill barely moves; the heat shows up at the peak and in the regions, not in the national average.

    Plan on the Weather That’s Coming

    Grids and household budgets are planned on past weather. Our forecast finds three gaps, and each needs a different fix.

    The gap we foundWhat to doWho

    1 · Grid

    Warming uses up about a fifth of the reserve cushion by 2025. In a hot summer, ERCOT falls to −1.3%, which means it runs short.

    Base reserve plans on forecast weather: add a warming adjustment to the 50/50 peak forecast. Start where margins are already at or below the reference level (ERCOT, MISO).
    NERC, regional grid operators, state utility commissions

    2 · Bills

    A low-income single person is over the affordable bar in 45 of 46 states, by about $300 a year. Heat adds only $17–33.

    Fix the structural gap: income-based rates or discounts, and LIHEAP money for summer cooling, not only winter heating.
    State commissions, utilities, LIHEAP (federal and state)

    3 · Homes

    North and West: no AC (WA 33%, ME 24%, MT 24% of homes). South: AC everywhere, but hot nights climb (FL 69 → 96, TX 46 → 75 a year).

    North and West: cooling where there is none, plus an indoor limit of about 82 °F in rentals. South: no shut-offs on cooling-required days, and cooling centers where reserves are thin.
    Cities, states, utilities

    The one lever that serves all three

    Efficient cooling for low-income homes: heat pumps in place of old AC and electric-resistance heat, plus weatherization and demand response at the peak. Lower peak use eases the grid, lower use eases the bill, and homes with no AC gain cooling.

    Why it is sustainable

    • Cuts peak demand instead of buying new peaker plants.
    • Heat pumps replace resistance and oil heat, so winter savings help pay.
    • Runs through programs that already exist.

    Limitations

    Warming is a modest part of grid risk to 2025. Here is what we assumed, what our data cannot see, and what would change the answer.

    Where the 2025 grid margin goes, median NERC area

    Percentage points of reserve margin lost

    Warming (our forecast)−1.6
    The plan’s own change (growth, retirements)−2.3
    One hot summer (about 1 in 10)−8.4

    What we assumed

    • NERC’s 50/50 peak forecasts do not already include warming.
    • Demand rises linearly with heat; the peak day’s high stands in for the peak hour.
    • A higher price in scarcity is only a scenario: hot years did not raise prices in 2002–2018.

    What our data cannot see

    • No humidity: dry-heat thresholds understate risk in humid places.
    • Airport stations miss city-centre heat; EIA-930 covers only 3.5 summers.
    • RECS income brackets approximate poverty; Alaska and Hawaii are left out.

    Outside our scope

    • The cold side: four regions hit their yearly peak in winter in 2016–2018. Grid risk runs both ways.
    • Load is what customers drew, so demand cut in emergencies does not show.

    What would change the conclusion

    Plans already include warming. Then the planning gap shrinks.

    The poor cut cooling more. Likely: bills fall but homes get hotter, so livability is worse than shown.

    Our forecast runs low. It was conservative on heat days, so warming’s effects are probably understated.

    Reproducibility & Openness

    One command rebuilds the submitted forecast byte for byte, and every number in this deck traces back to a script and a committed file.

    1

    The forecast

    • No randomness: same data in, same file out. Package versions pinned in requirements.txt.
    • Rebuilt from a clean copy of the repo: identical to submission.csv (5,880 rows, SHA-256 f1ba20cc…).
    • Training data pinned to revision bbbdebda… in shared.py, so later dataset updates cannot change it.
    pip install -r code/requirements.txt
python code/exp05b_daily_distribution.py
python code/check_submission.py submission.csv

    Under a minute on a laptop.

    2

    This deck

    1. Challenge data to 2018 + EIA, NERC, RECS, Census LACE
    2. policy_*.py: each writes its fixed settings to config.json before any result
    3. outputs/*/summary.md and CSVs, all committed
    4. export_deck_data.py → deck_data.js → these slides

    Every intermediate file is in the repo, so any step can be re-run on its own. Sources: data/README.md, data/policy_sources.md.

    3

    The data rule, in code

    • assert_cutoff stops any run that sees a year after 2018.
    • Each experiment's decision rule is written to decision.json before its forecast.
    • 2019–2025 observations: used only to score frozen forecasts after the fact, declared in the README, and kept out of these slides.
    • Agent rules in code/AGENTS.md; every AI step is listed in the README.

    Known gaps, stated up front

    Raw downloads. Agency files for the policy analysis are cached in data/raw/, which is not committed; a full rebuild re-downloads them.

    Census key. County matching needs a free Census API key. Its output is committed, so later steps run without one.

    Old scores. Held-back cdd scores in Experiments 3–7 predate the valid_flags fix. Forecasts are unaffected.

    Conclusion

    Not everyone will endure the heat on plans built for yesterday’s weather.

    Grids and household budgets are planned on past summers. Our forecast for 2019–2025, made only from data up to 2018, shows where that falls short.

    +48%

    The heat is uneven

    Hot nights rise by half and cooling-required days by a fifth, mostly in Texas, the Gulf Coast and the Southwest. Nights warm faster than days.

    ⅕

    The grid feels it first

    Warming uses up about a fifth of the planned reserve cushion. ERCOT, already below its reference margin, runs short in a hot summer.

    45 of 46

    Bills were already too high

    Low-income electricity bills were over the affordable bar in 45 of 46 states before any warming. Heat adds only $17–33 a year.

    So

    Plan reserves on forecast weather, fix affordability where it is structural, and bring cooling to homes that have none. One lever serves all three: efficient cooling for low-income homes, which lowers the peak, lowers the bill and makes homes livable.
